Kimetsu no Yaiba: Classic Structure Elevated by Ukiyo-e Art

A conservative plot disguised as a masterpiece thanks to unprecedented visual dynamism in modern animation.

Jose Jesus Lopez Fernandez14 min read
Imagen principal /img/articles/Kimetsu-no-yaiba-portada.jpg

Kimetsu no Yaiba — Koyoharu Gotouge. La danza de las hojas y la sangre.

In contrast to the postmodern nihilism of contemporary works, Koyoharu Gotouge's Kimetsu no Yaiba chose the diametrically opposite path: the structural purity of the classic monomyth. However, its massive success lies not in the complexity of its script, but in the mastery of its aesthetic execution.

The Heritage of Ukiyo-e in Visual Dynamism

The anime adaptation catapulted the work by incorporating elements of ukiyo-e (traditional Japanese woodblock prints) into the breathing technique effects. Katsushika Hokusai's waves came to life on Tanjiro's katana. This contrast between the hyperrealism of the backgrounds and the thick, stylized strokes of the elemental powers creates a unique visual language that breaks the monotony of the genre.

A Shinto Fairy Tale

Structurally, it is an extremely conservative work. Tanjiro Kamado is the embodiment of altruism, empathy, and pure resilience, lacking the moral ambiguity of other protagonists. The demons function as direct allegories for the loss of humanity and the social diseases of Taisho-era Japan, anchored in Shinto mythology.

"The strong must protect the weak. That is my duty."— Kyojuro Rengoku

Kimetsu no Yaiba does not invent anything new, but it perfects the wheel. It takes tragedy, folklore, and sword-swinging epics and elevates them through such overwhelming aesthetics that it turns a classic revenge story into a sweeping sensory experience.

Akaza: The Tragedy of Love That Cannot Be Forgotten

If there is one character in Kimetsu no Yaiba who elevates the work above its conservative structure, that character is Akaza. Upper Moon Three, one of the most powerful demons in Muzan's army, Akaza represents something that action fiction rarely permits itself: a villain whose cruelty is born directly from a love that the world could not let survive.

Before he was a demon, he was Soyama Hakuji. A child who stole to pay his sick father's debts. A young man who learned martial arts as his only means of survival. And a man who, for the first time in his life, found something worth living for: Keizō, the master who took him in without conditions, and Koyuki, his daughter — a young woman of fragile health and an impossible smile who taught him that the world could be gentle.

"What is happiness to you, Hakuji?"— Koyuki, to Hakuji

Hakuji promised to stop fighting. Promised to care for Koyuki and Keizō. And he kept that promise until a rival dojo poisoned the well and took them both from him in a single day. Hakuji's response was what only someone who has lost his entire world at once can give: he killed the sixty-seven members of the rival dojo with his bare hands. That naked violence, that rage which cannot distinguish between act and consequence, drew the attention of Muzan Kibutsuji, who offered him immortality — and with it, oblivion.

And this is where Akaza's psychology becomes truly tragic. As a demon, he forgets his past. He does not remember Koyuki. He does not remember Keizō. What he retains, unconsciously, is only the shape of his fists — he never uses any weapon, because he promised Koyuki he would stop hurting people — and an obsession with strength that is, in reality, the distorted version of a far deeper need: to protect those he loves. What in Hakuji was love has become in Akaza a hatred of weakness. The mechanism is the same; the object has changed.

John Bowlby's attachment theory describes how the trauma of losing attachment figures can completely reorganise a person's psychic structure. Akaza is a manga illustration of that process taken to its extreme: the loss of Koyuki and Keizō not only emotionally destroyed Hakuji, but erased his entire identity and replaced it with a being who can only relate to the world through domination. He cannot love again because loving cost him everything.

When Tanjiro defeats him and the memories return, the scene that Gotouge constructs is of a devastating beauty. Hakuji sees Koyuki waiting for him. He sees Keizō. And he chooses death — chooses to destroy his own head rather than continue as the demon that killed so many innocents — not as an act of cowardice, but as the only gesture of love left to him. It is, in the language of Japanese aesthetics, mono no aware taken to its purest expression: the sadness of things that could not be, the beauty of what is lost precisely because it was too fragile to last.

Akaza is not a great character because he is powerful. He is a great character because his violence has a direction: towards the pain he does not know how to name. And that — the inability to process grief without turning it into destruction — is one of the most human truths that fiction can portray.
